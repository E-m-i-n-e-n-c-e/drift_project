\begin{abstract}
Machine-learning models deployed on industrial sensor streams silently lose accuracy when the data-generating process changes, a phenomenon known as concept drift. We build an end-to-end streaming pipeline (Apache Kafka, Spark Structured Streaming, the River online-learning library, InfluxDB and Grafana) that monitors a live classifier and raises drift alarms. As the baseline we reproduce the detector benchmark of Sakurai et al.\ (DDM, EDDM, HDDM-A, HDDM-W) and add ADWIN. Our enhancement is a hybrid trigger that fuses a fast error-rate test (HDDM-A) with a window-mean test (ADWIN) using an OR rule and a refractory period. On 90 synthetic streams (abrupt, gradual, incremental drift) the hybrid attains the highest mean F1 (0.733 versus 0.672 for the best single detector; 0.786 versus 0.723 on 30 further unseen streams) at similar delay and false-alarm rate, but the improvement is not statistically significant under a Friedman/Nemenyi test. Confirmation-style (AND) fusion and EDDM-based hybrids performed worse than single detectors; we report these negative results. In a prequential study on a simulated IIoT stream, drift-triggered LightGBM retraining restores accuracy from 0.59 (static model) to 0.81--0.86. Results use synthetic and simulated data only; validation on IoTID20 and a measured end-to-end Kafka run remain future work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Industrial systems such as production lines, condition-monitoring networks and industrial intrusion-detection systems produce high-velocity streams whose statistics change with equipment wear, firmware updates, process reconfiguration and new attack behaviour. A classifier trained offline on historical data degrades under such \emph{concept drift}~\cite{gama2014survey}, which in predictive maintenance and security has safety and reliability consequences. A batch-only workflow finds the problem late, since accuracy is measured only after labels are aggregated and the model retrained offline. Detection must therefore run online, in bounded memory, and per sample.

\textbf{Problem statement.} Which drift-detection strategy offers the best trade-off between detection accuracy (F1), detection delay, false alarms and computational overhead on industrial streams, and can a simple hybrid of two detectors improve on the best single detector?

\textbf{Contributions.} (i) A working streaming pipeline in which a Kafka topic feeds an online learner with a drift detector whose outputs are written to InfluxDB and shown in Grafana. (ii) A reproduction of the detector-benchmark protocol of Sakurai et al.~\cite{sakurai2023} with a tolerance-window scoring rule and statistical testing. (iii) A hybrid HDDM-A/ADWIN trigger, with ablations against AND-fusion and EDDM-based pairings. (iv) A downstream study of drift-triggered retraining of a gradient-boosted model.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Base paper 1 (reproduced baseline).} Sakurai et al.~\cite{sakurai2023} benchmark the error-rate detectors DDM~\cite{gama2004}, EDDM~\cite{baena2006}, HDDM-A and HDDM-W~\cite{frias2015} on synthetic binary streams with controlled drift type, length and amplitude, using F1, detection time and detection delay with Friedman/Nemenyi testing. They conclude that no single detector dominates on all axes and that detector choice should depend on the drift scenario. We reproduce this protocol with River~\cite{montiel2021}.

\textbf{Base paper 2.} Yang and Shami~\cite{yang2023msana} propose a multi-stage online analytics framework for IIoT network data (MSANA) that pairs ADWIN~\cite{bifet2007} and EDDM as drift detectors and, on detection, re-selects features and retrains an online ensemble. It is evaluated on IoTID20 and CICIDS2017.

\textbf{Base paper 3.} Yang and Shami~\cite{yang2021lightweight} present a lightweight framework that combines LightGBM~\cite{ke2017} with an optimized adaptive sliding-window drift detector for resource-constrained IoT devices and shows that drift-triggered retraining is more accurate and cheaper than static or naively retrained models. We follow this ``detect, then retrain a lightweight model'' design for our downstream study.

\textbf{Gap.} Sakurai et al.\ evaluate detectors in isolation on synthetic streams; Yang and Shami use detector pairs inside larger pipelines, but the effect of the \emph{fusion rule} between two detectors is not isolated. We study that rule directly, on controlled ground truth and inside a running stream pipeline.

\section{System Architecture}
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the pipeline. A producer replays a simulated IIoT sensor stream (eight sensor channels with a normal/fault label and a known drift schedule) into the Kafka topic \texttt{sensors} at a configurable rate. The online scorer, a Python consumer, reads each record, predicts with a River Hoeffding Adaptive Tree, compares the prediction with the label (prequential test-then-train), updates the drift detector with the resulting error bit, and replaces the learner on an alarm. Per-record error, rolling error, alarm and ground-truth drift flags are written to InfluxDB as measurement \texttt{drift\_stream}, and a provisioned Grafana dashboard plots rolling error, alarms and true drift onsets with a five-second refresh. In parallel, a Spark Structured Streaming job reads the same topic and computes 10\,s windows (sliding 5\,s) of per-sensor mean, standard deviation and class rate. In the current implementation this branch writes to the console and does not feed the detector; it demonstrates the windowed feature layer but is not part of the scoring path.


\textbf{Why these tools.} Kafka provides a durable, replayable, ordered log that decouples the sensor source from the consumers, so the same recorded stream can drive the scorer and the Spark job independently and experiments can be repeated. River updates a model and a drift detector one sample at a time in constant memory, which is the property a batch library lacks. Spark Structured Streaming supplies event-time windows with watermarks for feature aggregation that would otherwise be hand-written. InfluxDB stores the resulting time series and Grafana renders them live, giving the monitoring view an operator needs. A single script reading a CSV once would compute the same detector scores but would not exercise transport, windowing, storage or visualization, nor show behaviour on a live feed.

\section{Proposed Enhancement}
Error-rate detectors differ in what they are sensitive to: DDM/HDDM-A test a running mean of the error bit against its historical minimum, HDDM-W applies exponentially weighted moving averages, EDDM monitors the distance between consecutive errors, and ADWIN adaptively shrinks a window whenever two sub-windows have significantly different means~\cite{bifet2007}. We combine two detectors with different mechanisms:
\begin{equation}
\text{alarm}_t=\big(f_t\lor s_t\big)\land(t-t_{\text{last}}>R),
\end{equation}
where $f_t$ is HDDM-A, $s_t$ is ADWIN, $t_{\text{last}}$ is the previous emitted alarm and $R$ is a refractory period. The OR rule raises recall, since the two tests fire on different samples, and the refractory period merges the bursts of alarms that follow a single drift, protecting precision. The pairing and $R=500$ were chosen during exploratory runs on the evaluation seeds 0--29, so results on those seeds are optimistically biased. A later sweep over $R\in\{100,200,300,500,800\}$ on separate seeds 1000--1019 (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}) and a confirmatory benchmark on untouched seeds 2000--2029 (Table~\ref{tab:conf}) were run to check this. We also implement a confirmation rule ($f$ and $s$ within $k=300$ samples of each other) and the pairing HDDM-W/EDDM as ablations. Both detectors are used with River's default parameters.

\section{Implementation}
The code is Python~3.12 with River~0.26.1, LightGBM~4.7.0, NumPy~2.4.4, SciPy~1.17.1, scikit-learn~1.8.0 and pandas~3.0.2. The streaming infrastructure is configured in \texttt{docker-compose.yml} with Apache Kafka~3.7.0 in KRaft mode, InfluxDB~2.7 and Grafana~10.4.2; the Spark job targets Spark~3.5 with the Kafka connector. Kafka clients use \texttt{kafka-python} and the InfluxDB writer uses \texttt{influxdb-client}.

\textbf{Synthetic drift streams.} Following the protocol of~\cite{sakurai2023}, each stream is a binary error indicator of length 10\,000 with three drifts at positions 2\,500, 5\,000 and 7\,500. At each drift the error probability rises by 0.12 from a base of 0.10. Abrupt drift switches instantly; incremental drift ramps the probability linearly over 500 samples; gradual drift draws each sample from the new concept with a linearly increasing probability over 500 samples. We generate 30 seeds per drift type (90 streams).

\textbf{Simulated IIoT stream.} For the downstream study we generate 20\,000 samples with eight features and a binary label from a linear boundary plus an interaction term and noise. The boundary changes abruptly at samples 5\,000 and 10\,000 and gradually over 1\,500 samples from 15\,000, so drift is real concept drift.

\textbf{Downstream learners.} The first 1\,000 samples train the initial LightGBM model. Evaluation is prequential in micro-batches of 25. For drift-triggered variants the detector receives each error bit; after an alarm the pipeline waits 250 samples and retrains on the latest 500 labelled samples. LightGBM is not an online learner, so this delayed retraining is an explicit design choice. River's Adaptive Random Forest (five trees) and Hoeffding Adaptive Tree serve as true online baselines.

\section{Experimental Setup and Results}
\textbf{Scoring rule.} A true drift onset $t$ is detected if the first alarm falls in $[t,\,t+w+300)$ and before the next onset, where $w$ is the drift width. Every other alarm is a false alarm and an unmatched onset is a miss. We report F1, mean detection delay over detected drifts, false alarms per 1\,000 samples, per-sample detector time and peak allocated memory (\texttt{tracemalloc}, one stream). Detectors are compared with the Friedman test on per-stream F1 ($N=90$ blocks, $k=9$ detectors) and the Nemenyi critical difference~\cite{demsar2006}.

\textbf{Detector benchmark.} Table~\ref{tab:overall} and Fig.~\ref{fig:bench} give the results. The Friedman test rejects equal performance ($\chi^2=410.1$, $p<10^{-80}$); the Nemenyi critical difference is 1.27 rank units. The hybrid has the best mean rank (2.23) and F1 (0.733), ahead of HDDM-A (2.89, 0.672) and ADWIN (3.17, 0.611). The rank differences from HDDM-A (0.66) and ADWIN (0.94) are below the critical difference, so the hybrid is \emph{not} statistically distinguishable from these two. Differences to HDDM-W, DDM, EDDM and all ablations exceed it.

\textbf{Negative results.} Confirmation (AND) fusion lowered F1 for both pairings tested (0.370 and 0.417), because requiring agreement between a fast and a slow detector drops recall (0.23--0.66) and lengthens delay while the false-alarm rate barely improves. Pairing HDDM-W with EDDM inherited EDDM's high false-alarm rate under an OR rule and was worse than either HDDM-W alone or the pairing with ADWIN.

\textbf{Downstream adaptation.} Table~\ref{tab:down} and Fig.~\ref{fig:down} show prequential accuracy on the simulated IIoT stream (one stream, seed 0). A static LightGBM falls to 0.586 accuracy overall (0.497 after the first drift). Every drift-triggered variant recovers to between 0.81 and 0.86. The hybrid trigger has the best detection F1 (0.857, tied with HDDM-A) and a delay of 220 samples versus 268 for HDDM-A, but its overall accuracy (0.811) is lower than ADWIN's (0.863) and River's Hoeffding Adaptive Tree (0.874). EDDM triggers 37 alarms and 27 retrains for three true drifts. The online Hoeffding Adaptive Tree needs no external detector and was the most accurate strategy here.

\section{Discussion and Inferences}
\textbf{What the results say.} Detectors that watch a running mean (HDDM-A, ADWIN) were the most reliable on our synthetic streams; sensitive detectors (EDDM, HDDM-W) reacted faster but produced many more false alarms, so under an F1 that penalizes every extra alarm they rank lower. The OR-fusion of two mean-based detectors with different mechanisms increased recall (0.76 versus 0.69 for HDDM-A alone, from held-out tuning) while the refractory period kept false alarms at 0.097 per 1\,000 samples. The hybrid's gain is modest and, under the Nemenyi test, not significant against HDDM-A or ADWIN. It costs about one microsecond more per sample than HDDM-A, negligible next to any Kafka hop.

\textbf{Relation to base paper 1.} Sakurai et al.\ report EDDM as the most accurate detector overall and HDDM-W as the best trade-off. Our results differ: EDDM has the highest recall and shortest delay but the lowest F1. We attribute this mainly to our scoring rule (all extra alarms are false alarms), our one-sided error-rate increases, and different stream parameters; we did not tune any baseline detector. We do not claim to have reproduced their ranking.

\textbf{Downstream.} Drift detection is worth doing: static LightGBM lost about 40 accuracy points. However, better detection F1 did not translate into better accuracy in our single run, since accuracy also depends on when retraining happens and how much post-drift data is available (250-sample wait, 500-sample window), and on false-alarm retrains that happen to be harmless. An online tree with built-in drift handling outperformed all retrain-on-alarm variants, so a detector-plus-retrain design must be justified by other benefits (model choice, cost, interpretability) rather than accuracy alone.

\textbf{Threats to validity.} (1) All detector results use synthetic Bernoulli error streams; real classifier errors are autocorrelated. (2) The fusion pair and $R$ were chosen on the evaluation seeds 0--29, and only the hybrid was tuned while the baselines run at River defaults, which favors the hybrid; the unseen-seed run (Table~\ref{tab:conf}) shows the same ordering but does not remove the untuned-baseline advantage. (3) The 300-sample margin in the tolerance window and the choice of drift amplitude are ours. (4) Mean delay is computed over detected drifts only, so detectors that miss drifts have optimistic delay. (5) The downstream study uses one stream and one seed. (6) IoTID20 and CICIDS2017 were not used, so no claim is made about real network traffic; real datasets carry no drift labels and would need injected drift. (7) ADWIN is two-sided and can fire when a freshly reset learner \emph{improves}, which likely explains some of the extra alarms in the live run. (8) The Spark branch is not on the scoring path, and end-to-end Kafka-to-Grafana latency and throughput were not measured.


\textbf{Deployment considerations.} The detectors add microseconds per sample and a few kilobytes of state (Table~\ref{tab:overall}), so on the detector side the cost is dominated by the classifier and by transport, not by drift detection. Two practical points follow from the live run. First, a detector fed by a learner that has just been reset sees a falling error rate, and two-sided tests such as ADWIN can treat improvement as change; guarding the detector for a warm-up period or using a one-sided variant would remove this class of alarms. Second, in a plant an alarm should trigger a review or a controlled retrain rather than an unattended model swap, so the alarm precision reported here matters more than the delay.

\section{Reproducibility}
The repository provides the commands \texttt{python -m drift.benchmark --seeds 30} (exploratory seeds), \texttt{python -m drift.benchmark --seeds 30 --start 2000 --out results\_heldout} (confirmatory seeds), \texttt{python -m drift.tune\_hybrid} (refractory sweep), \texttt{python -m drift.adaptive} (downstream study) and \texttt{python -m streaming.run\_local} (in-process pipeline), plus \texttt{docker compose up -d}, \texttt{python -m streaming.consumer} and \texttt{python -m streaming.producer} for the Kafka--InfluxDB--Grafana path. All random seeds are fixed. Wall-clock timings depend on the machine and vary between runs.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We built a streaming drift-monitoring pipeline and compared error-rate and window-based detectors on controlled synthetic drift. A hybrid of HDDM-A and ADWIN with a refractory period gave the best mean F1 and rank, though not significantly better than its components, while confirmation-based hybrids and EDDM-based pairings failed. Drift-triggered retraining recovers most of the accuracy a static model loses. Future work: evaluate on IoTID20/CICIDS2017 with injected drift, tune all detectors on equal footing, use one-sided ADWIN or a warm-up guard after learner resets, route Spark window features into the detector, and measure end-to-end latency, throughput and drift-recovery time on the Kafka pipeline.
